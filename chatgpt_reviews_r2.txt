The strongest part of the paper

The paper is substantially better than I expected from the title alone.

The main scientific question is excellent:

Can we determine before training whether two information sources will complement, duplicate, or interfere with one another?

That is more interesting than simply proposing MomentAux.

The major issues I would address

1. The prospective claim is currently stronger than the prospective experiment. This is the single most important issue. The abstract says the fusion outcome “can be anticipated before training,” and the introduction makes prediction-before-combination one of the paper’s central contributions. But most of the stack/substitute/tax taxonomy is established retrospectively. The manuscript itself admits that only the procedure in Section “A procedure for deciding whether to fuse” was actually called before training. Worse, that demonstration uses quantities measured on the test split. I would add a genuine prospective experiment. Choose perhaps 10–20 previously untouched source pairs across several datasets/backbones, calculate all diagnostics using validation only, commit the predicted outcome, train the combinations, and reveal test performance afterward. This one experiment could increase the paper’s credibility dramatically.
2. “Currency” needs one operational definition that does not require seeing the combination. There is currently a subtle logical inconsistency. The fusion section says two sources have the same currency when their G values agree and the combination’s G does not exceed the better individual source. But if I need the combination’s G, then I have already trained the combination, so this cannot be the quantity used to decide whether to fuse beforehand. Later, the algorithm instead uses G_A, G_B, performance similarity and whether both arms improve the same images. That is closer to what you need. I would define currency exclusively using single-source information. For example, construct a source similarity vector from G, corrected-image overlap, CKA/representation similarity and perhaps invariance statistics, and then prospectively predict the fusion outcome. The combination should only be used for evaluating the prediction, never defining the predictor.
3. “Tax” is currently presented too much as a fusion category when your own experiment shows it is predominantly a schedule effect. This section contains a very important result: at \lambda_0=1, injecting MomentAux into ImageNet-pretrained features produces enormous degradation; at \lambda_0\le0.3, the degradation essentially disappears. Therefore the scientifically precise conclusion is not simply \text{prior}+\text{mature initialization}\Rightarrow\text{tax}. It is closer to \text{strong early representational shaping}+\text{mature initialization}\Rightarrow\text{interference}. The manuscript itself says: “The tax is a property of the schedule, not of the fusion.” That sentence conflicts somewhat with presenting “tax” alongside stack and substitute as a general fusion class. I would rename this category interference under incompatible/over-strong shaping and make auxiliary strength part of the condition.
4. “Different currencies stack” is not general enough yet to be written as a universal rule. Your own off-selection convolutional test is the key problem: prior + augmentation stacks at only 1 of 6 convolutional cells, while it works convincingly on the attention backbones. That is actually an interesting finding, but it means architecture is a moderator. The manuscript already acknowledges this, but the abstract/algorithm/taxonomy remain stronger than the detailed evidence. I would formulate the conclusion as something closer to: non-overlapping representational contributions are necessary but not always sufficient for constructive training-time fusion; compatibility with architecture and intervention strength moderates whether the potential complementarity is realized. That is less catchy but much harder for a reviewer to attack.
5. The CIFAR-100 test-set selection issue is serious. The paper handles it admirably transparently, but transparency does not remove the methodological problem. MomentAux’s target, tap depth, \lambda_0, head normalization and loss were selected on the CIFAR-100 test set. Therefore CIFAR-100 is effectively a development dataset. I would explicitly rename it as such throughout the manuscript instead of continuing to describe its results like ordinary test results. You do not necessarily need to retrain all 9,402 runs. A more feasible solution is to designate CIFAR-100 as development and introduce a small but rigorous confirmatory benchmark on completely untouched datasets/backbones. Those confirmatory experiments should carry the strongest claims.
6. Ironically, the matched-label-budget finding may be stronger than the “law” currently given the most space. This is one of the most important observations I found while reviewing the manuscript. Your headline decomposition is \Delta=G+\mathrm{readout}(\mathrm{base}), but the equality itself is definitional, as you correctly acknowledge. Then you discover that when the frozen-feature evaluation uses the practitioner’s actual label budget, most of the negative readout branch disappears and \Delta and G agree to 0.17 points on average across 30 cells and seven datasets. That is remarkably clean. I would elevate this result. A much stronger practical message is: a cheap matched-budget linear probe can accurately estimate whether an intervention’s representational gain will translate into end-to-end gain. Conversely, the full-label sign law has only 47.5% resolvable coverage, is 94.9% correct below the crossing but only 67.4% above it, and its negative branch is substantially induced by the mismatch between training and evaluation label budgets. I would make the sign law a secondary explanatory result rather than the conceptual center.
7. The comparator set should be modernized slightly for a 2026 submission. SimCLR, SimSiam and DINO are useful because they represent different SSL mechanisms, but reviewers may ask why a 2026 benchmark does not contain at least one masked/predictive modern SSL representative. You do not need to repeat the entire grid. One carefully chosen modern comparator would suffice—MAE or I-JEPA would make sense conceptually because their “currency” may differ from contrastive invariance. MAE is a standard masked-image pretraining method and I-JEPA explicitly learns through latent prediction rather than contrastive augmentation invariance.  This would actually help test the currency hypothesis rather than merely satisfy reviewers.
8. There is a highly relevant related-work paper missing from the conceptual discussion. Ericsson, Gouk and Hospedales explicitly study which invariances SSL learns and show that downstream tasks benefit differently from those invariances; they also report that fusing complementary representations improves transfer across diverse tasks. That is very close to your “different currencies stack” motivation.  You should cite it and explain the distinction clearly: they demonstrate complementary invariances and representation fusion; you are attempting to build a prospective, cost-normalized decision rule for whether two sources will be complementary, redundant or interfering.

My likely reviewer decision

Were I reviewing the exact manuscript you uploaded, my recommendation would be:

Major Revision — potentially strong acceptance after revision.

I would not reject it because the experimental evidence is inadequate. The opposite is true. I would ask for revision because the central conceptual claim has not yet been subjected to the same standard of clean validation that the authors apply to the rest of the manuscript.

The single highest-value addition would be this:

\boxed{
\textbf{A clean prospective fusion-prediction benchmark}
}

Use untouched dataset/backbone/source pairs. Compute the source diagnostics on validation data. Freeze the prediction. Train the combination. Evaluate once on test.

If that experiment demonstrates that the source-alone diagnostic predicts stack vs. substitute vs. interference with convincing accuracy across held-out combinations, I think the manuscript becomes a genuinely strong Information Fusion paper, because then the contribution is no longer merely a very large retrospective benchmark—it becomes an experimentally validated decision framework for fusion.

And I would seriously consider reorganizing the paper around that claim. The material to support a very strong Q1 submission is already here; the issue is primarily claim hierarchy, clean prospective validation, and focus, rather than a lack of experiments.
